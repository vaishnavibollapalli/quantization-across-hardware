Kestrel Valley Community Microgrid: 2025 Annual Operations Report

The Kestrel Valley Community Microgrid completed its fourth full year of operation in 2025. The system serves 1,240 households and 38 businesses across the town and the surrounding farm roads. Generation comes from a 4.2 MW solar array spread over three sites, a 1.5 MW wind installation on Harrow Ridge, and a lithium iron phosphate battery system. During 2025, the microgrid supplied 71 percent of the town's total electricity demand, up from 58 percent in 2024. The operations team attributes most of this improvement to the second battery bank, commissioned in March 2025, which raised total storage from 3 MWh to 6 MWh. With more storage available, surplus midday solar energy could be held for the evening peak instead of being curtailed or exported at low prices. Purchases from the regional grid fell by roughly one third compared with the previous year. The microgrid remained connected to the regional utility throughout the year and continued to import power during long stretches of cloudy, low-wind weather. This report summarizes generation, storage, reliability, costs, community programs, and the plans approved by the cooperative board for 2026.

Generation followed a familiar seasonal pattern. January and February were the weakest months for solar output, as short days and repeated snowfall reduced production; crews cleared the two most accessible arrays after each major storm, but the hillside array at Pell Orchard often stayed covered for several days. Wind partly offset the shortfall: the Harrow Ridge turbines produced their highest monthly total in February, at 498 MWh. Solar output climbed steadily through spring and peaked in June at 812 MWh, the highest single-month figure in the system's history. July and August were slightly lower because of haze from distant wildfires, which reduced irradiance on several afternoons. Autumn brought a gradual decline in solar production and a return of stronger winds in late October and November. Across the full year, solar contributed about 64 percent of local generation and wind about 36 percent. The team noted that the combination of the two sources remains valuable, since the windiest weeks rarely coincide with the sunniest ones. Forecasting improved as well: day-ahead solar forecasts were within ten percent of actual output on 83 percent of days, compared with 71 percent in 2024.

The expanded battery system completed 287 full equivalent charge cycles in 2025. Typical daily operation involved charging from surplus solar between late morning and mid-afternoon, then discharging between 5 p.m. and 10 p.m., when household demand is highest. Annual capacity testing in December measured a 2.1 percent loss of usable capacity in the original battery bank, which is within the manufacturer's expected range for a system of its age. The newer bank showed no measurable loss. The most significant battery incident occurred in July, when a cooling fan in the original enclosure failed during a heat wave. Internal temperatures rose above the recommended limit, and the battery management system automatically reduced the discharge rate by half until a replacement fan was installed two days later. No cells were damaged. Following the incident, the team added remote temperature alerts that notify on-call staff by text message, and it now keeps two spare fans on site. Staff also began a quarterly inspection of air filters, which had become clogged with pollen and dust more quickly than expected during the dry summer months.

The microgrid recorded three unplanned outages in 2025, compared with five in 2024. The first, in January, lasted 22 minutes and was caused by ice loading on a distribution line near the school. The second, in May, lasted 48 minutes after a vehicle struck a utility pole on Route 9. The longest outage occurred on August 14, when a lightning strike damaged equipment at the main inverter station and left part of the town without power for 3 hours and 40 minutes. In each case, the microgrid's islanding controls worked as designed for the sections that were not physically damaged: those areas disconnected from the regional grid and continued operating on local solar and battery power. During a separate regional blackout in September, which lasted more than six hours, the entire town remained powered by the microgrid alone. Residents and local businesses widely cited this event as the clearest demonstration of the system's value. The team has since installed additional surge protection at the inverter station and is reviewing the grounding design with an outside engineering firm.

Average residential electricity rates in Kestrel Valley were 13.8 cents per kilowatt-hour in 2025, compared with a regional average of 16.1 cents. Commercial rates averaged 11.9 cents. The cooperative's total operating expenses were 2.3 million dollars, of which maintenance accounted for about 18 percent and debt service on the second battery bank accounted for about 27 percent. Revenue exceeded expenses by a modest margin, and the board placed the surplus in a reserve fund for equipment replacement. Lower purchases from the regional grid were the largest single source of savings. However, insurance premiums rose by 12 percent after the lightning incident, and the cost of replacement inverter parts was higher than budgeted. The board decided to keep rates unchanged for 2026 rather than lowering them, in order to rebuild the reserve fund more quickly. A rate review is scheduled for the end of 2026 once the effects of the planned storage expansion are known. The cooperative also received a state grant that covered part of the cost of the forecasting software upgrade completed in the spring.

Community programs continued to grow alongside the physical system. The heat pump rebate program, which offers up to 2,500 dollars toward replacing oil or propane heating, was used by 214 households in 2025, bringing the program's three-year total to 506 homes. Because heat pumps increase winter electricity demand, the operations team now includes expected heat pump adoption in its seasonal load forecasts. The cooperative also installed 12 public electric vehicle charging ports, eight at the town hall parking lot and four at the grocery store on Main Street. Charging prices are lower between 10 a.m. and 3 p.m. to encourage drivers to charge when solar output is highest. A volunteer energy coach program trained 15 residents to help neighbors read their bills and understand time-of-use pricing. The high school's environmental science class used real-time data from the microgrid for a semester-long project, and two students completed summer internships with the operations team. Attendance at the annual member meeting reached a record 341 people.

Several challenges slowed work during the year. A firmware update to the inverters in April contained a bug that caused occasional false fault readings; the issue did not affect power delivery, but it generated hundreds of unnecessary alarms until the manufacturer released a corrected version in June. A replacement transformer for the Pell Orchard array was delivered 19 weeks later than promised because of supply chain delays, and the array operated at reduced capacity during that period. Staffing was also a constraint: the operations team has four full-time employees, and one position was vacant from May to September. Several projects, including the planned upgrade of the customer billing portal, were postponed to 2026. Finally, a proposal to add a third solar site near the river was withdrawn after a wetlands survey found that the location was home to a protected amphibian species. The board has asked staff to identify alternative sites, with a preference for rooftops and land that has already been developed, such as the parking area behind the community center.

For 2026, the cooperative board approved three main initiatives. First, the microgrid will add 2 MWh of battery storage in a new building next to the inverter station, bringing total capacity to 8 MWh. In its November meeting, the board voted to name this new building the Marigold Storage Annex, after the wildflowers planted along its access road. Second, the cooperative will begin a vehicle-to-grid pilot with the school district, using four electric school buses that can return power to the microgrid during summer months when school is not in session. Third, staff will complete the delayed billing portal upgrade and add a feature that shows each member how much of their electricity came from local sources each month. The board set a target of supplying 80 percent of the town's electricity locally by the end of 2026. Staff caution that reaching the target depends on weather, on timely equipment delivery, and on how quickly winter demand grows as more households switch to heat pumps. Progress will be reported at quarterly member meetings.

Ashgrove County Public Library System: 2025 Annual Report

The Ashgrove County Public Library System operates seven branches serving about 186,000 residents. In 2025, the system recorded 1.12 million in-person visits, an increase of 9 percent over 2024. Total circulation reached 2.4 million items, of which 41 percent were digital, including e-books, e-audiobooks, and streaming films. The number of active library card holders rose to 97,500. Library leadership attributes most of the growth in visits to the reopening of the Central Branch in April, after a renovation that kept the building closed for 14 months. During the closure, many regular visitors had shifted to smaller branches or to digital services, and the reopening brought a large share of them back. Digital borrowing continued to grow even after the Central Branch reopened, suggesting that many patrons now use both physical and digital collections rather than choosing one. This report covers the renovation, collections, public programs, staffing, finances, the challenges the system faced during the year, and the plans approved by the library board for 2026. All figures are for the calendar year unless otherwise noted.

The Central Branch renovation cost 18.6 million dollars. Most of the funding came from a bond measure approved by county voters in 2022, which passed with 61 percent of the vote; private donations contributed a further 2.1 million dollars. The renovated building includes a makerspace with three 3D printers, a laser cutter, and sewing and electronics stations, as well as 12 bookable study rooms. The children's wing was doubled in size and now includes a dedicated story-time room with acoustic panels. A new rooftop reading garden, open from April through October, quickly became one of the most photographed spots in the city. Solar panels on the south side of the roof, combined with LED lighting and an upgraded ventilation system, have reduced the building's electricity use by about 30 percent compared with the old building. Opening day on April 12 drew about 4,800 visitors, and staff issued more than 600 new library cards that weekend. The makerspace was fully booked for its first six weeks, and the library has since added evening sessions to meet demand.

The library added about 38,000 new physical items to its collections in 2025 and removed roughly 45,000 outdated or damaged items, most of which were sold at book sales or sent for recycling. E-book loans reached 640,000, the highest annual total so far. In May, the library launched a Library of Things collection with 220 items that can be borrowed like books, including telescopes, sewing machines, board games, power tools, and portable Wi-Fi hotspots. The 150 hotspots were the most requested items in the collection, and the average wait for one was about three weeks by the end of the year. To reduce the wait, the library shortened the hotspot loan period from three weeks to two. Staff also expanded the world languages collection, adding titles in Spanish, Vietnamese, Korean, and Amharic based on patron requests and census data. A new catalog search feature now shows whether an item is on the shelf at each branch in real time, which reduced phone calls asking about availability by about a quarter.

The library offered about 6,900 public programs in 2025, with total attendance of 142,000. The summer reading challenge enrolled a record 11,300 children, and 64 percent of them completed the challenge by reading or listening for at least 20 hours. The adult literacy program served 280 learners with the help of 96 volunteer tutors, and 41 learners passed a high school equivalency exam during the year. The job and career center held about 1,900 one-on-one sessions covering resumes, online applications, and interview practice; 214 participants later reported that they had found a job. Technology classes for older adults, covering topics such as video calls and avoiding online scams, were among the fastest growing programs, with attendance up 38 percent. Teen programs included a weekly coding club and a youth advisory board that helped plan the new makerspace schedule. Staff noted that evening and weekend programs consistently drew larger audiences than weekday daytime sessions, a pattern that is shaping the program calendar for 2026.

The library system employs 164 staff members, of whom 118 work full time. Staff turnover was 8 percent, down from 13 percent in 2024, which leadership credits partly to a new pay scale adopted in January. Starting in September, four branches extended their hours to include Sunday afternoons, a change that had been one of the most common requests in patron surveys. In June, the library took delivery of a new electric bookmobile, which replaced a diesel vehicle that had been in service for 19 years. The bookmobile now makes 23 scheduled stops each week, including senior centers, rural schools, and two apartment complexes that are far from any branch. Because the new vehicle has a wheelchair lift and onboard Wi-Fi, it can also host small computer help sessions. Volunteers contributed about 21,000 hours of service during the year, mostly shelving, tutoring, and helping run the book sales. Staff training focused on customer service, digital privacy, and supporting patrons in crisis, in partnership with a local social services agency.

The library's operating budget for 2025 was 21.4 million dollars. About 78 percent came from the county, 9 percent from the state, and the rest from grants, donations, and other sources; competitive grants totaled 1.3 million dollars. In January 2025, the library eliminated overdue fines on all materials. Fines had previously brought in about 310,000 dollars per year, but staff found that the cost of collecting them and the number of patrons who stopped using the library because of unpaid fines outweighed the revenue. After the change, 4,100 previously blocked accounts were reactivated, and the share of items returned within a month of their due date rose to 96 percent. Patrons are still charged for items that are lost or damaged. Personnel costs made up about 64 percent of spending, collections about 14 percent, and facilities and technology most of the remainder. The board approved a modest increase in the collections budget for 2026, with most of the new funding going to digital materials and the Library of Things.

The year also brought several setbacks. In February, a roof leak at the Riverside Branch damaged part of the collection and closed the branch for five weeks while repairs were made. In August, the heating and cooling system at the Northgate Branch failed during a heat wave, and the branch closed early on six days until a replacement unit was installed. In October, the library was targeted by a phishing attack in which a staff member's email account was compromised. The IT team contained the incident within six hours, and an outside review found that no patron data had been exposed; however, the online catalog was taken offline for two days as a precaution. The library has since required two-factor authentication for all staff accounts. Parking remains a frequent complaint at the Central Branch, especially on Saturdays, and the library is negotiating with the county to share a nearby government parking garage on weekends. Finally, rising costs for e-book licenses continue to strain the digital budget.

For 2026, the library board approved plans to open an eighth branch, the Willow Creek Branch, in a former bank building on Orchard Avenue. Renovation is scheduled to finish in October 2026. The branch's children's reading room will be called the Lantern Room, a name chosen in a vote by about 3,200 local students. The library also plans to expand the Library of Things to about 400 items, add more hotspots to shorten the waiting list, and launch an online homework help service staffed by tutors until 9 p.m. on school nights. The board set a goal of 1.2 million in-person visits for 2026. Staff caution that meeting the goal depends on the Willow Creek Branch opening on schedule and on resolving the parking problem at the Central Branch. A community survey will be conducted in the spring to guide decisions about branch hours and new services, and the results will be presented at the board's June meeting.
